THE KIN-STRIFE

How a marriage of love became a war for the throne, and how the throne's revenge on itself took six hundred years to finish.

I. Before the Storm

In the year 1250 of the Third Age, Gondor was strong, prosperous, and quietly worried about its neighbors. The regent Minalcar — who had ruled Gondor in all but name for a decade already, and would not formally take the crown and the name Rómendacil II for another fifty-four years — sent his son Valacar north as ambassador to Vidugavia, the most powerful of the Northmen chieftains who called himself King of Rhovanion. It was ordinary statecraft: learn the Northmen's language, their customs, their politics. Keep the alliance that had helped Gondor beat back the Easterlings warm.

Valacar did more than learn the customs. He fell in love with Vidugavia's daughter, Vidumavi, and married her. In 1255, she bore him a son. His mother called him Vinitharya, in her own tongue; his father, thinking of the crown he would one day carry south, called him Eldacar.

For five more years the family stayed in the North. Then, in 1260, Minalcar recalled his son home — and Valacar brought his wife and children back to Osgiliath with him. Gondor mostly welcomed them. Vidumavi learned the language and ways of her adopted country so well that she took a Sindarin name of her own, Galadwen. Chroniclers who saw her described her as fair, noble, and courageous — qualities she visibly passed to her son.

Not everyone was charmed. A hard core of Dúnedain nobility, especially in the southern and coastal fiefs, never accepted her. Their objection wasn't really about Vidumavi personally. It was about what she represented: lesser blood in the line of kings descended from Númenor itself. They were, they said, worried about lifespan — Northmen lived Mannish years, seventy or eighty, not the centuries a pure Dúnadan line was still capable of. What they were more quietly worried about was status. And when Vidumavi died – in 1332, or perhaps 1344, the two surviving copies of the royal record disagree by twelve years and neither Tolkien nor his son ever reconciled them — without ever becoming Queen, her critics treated it as proof they'd been right all along.

Valacar became King of Gondor in 1366. His son and heir was, by then, over a hundred years old and had shown no more sign of aging than any other Dúnadan lord. It didn't matter. The resentment had stopped being about facts a long time before that.

II. The Realm Divides

When Eldacar took the throne in 1432, the smouldering resentment of his father's reign caught fire in earnest. His chief rival was his own second cousin: Castamir, Captain of Ships, a popular and capable naval commander whose power base was the coast — Pelargir and Umbar, the fiefs that lived by the fleet rather than the land. Castamir had never been especially close to the throne by blood. What he had instead was purity of lineage, ambition, and a navy.

Gondor split along a fault line that was partly bloodline politics and partly geography. The coastal south rallied to Castamir. The interior and the north — Calenardhon, Anórien, Ithilien, the older heartland of the kingdom — mostly stayed loyal to the king they already had, whatever his mother's people. In 1437, five years into a rebellion that had never fully gone away, Castamir moved from politics to war.

III. The Burning of Osgiliath

Castamir marched on the capital with the fleet and the coastal levies behind him. Osgiliath had never been built to withstand a siege from its own side — it relied on Minas Anor and Minas Ithil, the fortress-cities to either side, for real defense. It fell within months.

What happened during the sack was, by every surviving account, worse than the military situation required. Osgiliath burned. The Dome of Stars — a marvel of Númenórean stonework built to house one of the seven palantíri brought out of the West — was destroyed in the fire, and the Osgiliath-stone itself was lost in the waters of the Anduin, never recovered. It remains lost today, one of only two palantíri whose fate is known for certain and the only one destroyed rather than merely mislaid.

Eldacar's eldest son and heir, Ornendil, did not escape the city. Castamir had him captured and, when Osgiliath fell, put to death. It was meant as a message: this line ends here. Eldacar himself got out, fleeing north through Ithilien and on into Rhovanion, back to the mother's kin who had started all this trouble in the first place.

Castamir took the throne he had been so sure was rightfully his. He held it for ten years. He would come to wish, probably, that he had never wanted it so badly.

IV. Ten Years, Two Kings

What Castamir did with the crown undid him more thoroughly than Eldacar's armies ever could have on their own. He was, by every account, a genuinely capable naval commander — and a ruler with almost no interest in the parts of his kingdom that weren't coastline. He tried to move the capital itself from Osgiliath to Pelargir, a port city, a plan that read to the interior provinces as exactly what it was: contempt for the land he'd burned a city to inherit. Anórien and Ithilien, which had no particular love for Eldacar to begin with, found they had even less for the man who'd taken his place. Cruelty did the rest. The sack of Osgiliath and the killing of a teenage heir were not forgotten, and Castamir showed no instinct for the kind of generosity that might have bought forgiveness anyway.

Eldacar, meanwhile, spent the decade doing the one thing his enemies had always denied he was entitled to: being Gondorian. He gathered his mother's kinsfolk, the Northmen who had served in Gondor's armies for two generations, and — this is the detail that matters — a steadily growing number of native Dúnedain from the northern provinces, lords who had never much cared about the purity argument and cared quite a lot about the burned capital and the murdered prince.

By 1447, the exile who'd been driven out as a half-breed had a Gondorian army. The Usurper who'd been crowned as the champion of pure blood had alienated most of the people whose purity he claimed to be defending.

V. The Crossings of Erui

Eldacar came south in 1447 with his army from Rhovanion, and as he crossed into Gondor proper the provinces he'd once been told he had no right to rule — Calenardhon, Anórien, Ithilien — rose and joined him. Castamir marched to meet him in Lebennin, at the river Erui, with whatever force his ten unpopular years had left him.

The battle at the Crossings of Erui was not a clean victory. The chronicles are unusually blunt about the cost: much of the best blood of Gondor was spilled there, on both sides — men who, whatever banner they fought under, were descendants of the same Númenórean stock the whole war had supposedly been fought to protect. Eldacar found Castamir in the fighting and killed him himself.

It should have ended there. It didn't, quite.

VI. What Was Lost at Umbar

Castamir's sons survived the battle and fled with the remnant of his loyalists to Pelargir, the port city their father had wanted for a capital. Eldacar besieged them by land through the following year, but he had no fleet — Castamir had commanded the navy for a decade, and the navy's loyalty had not transferred with the crown. In 1448 the rebels broke the siege by sea and sailed for Umbar, the great haven far to the south, taking every ship that would carry them.

Gondor lost Umbar that year and would not hold it again for four hundred years. Castamir's descendants built an independent lordship there, hostile to the throne that had killed their grandfather, and became the Corsairs of Umbar — a name that would go on troubling Gondor's coasts for the rest of the Third Age, all the way down to a battle fought at Pelargir in the War of the Ring itself, when the ships that Aragorn commandeered on the Paths of the Dead were, by ancestry, the great-great-grandchildren of this exact rebellion.

VII. The Long Shadow

Here is the part that doesn't get told as often, because it isn't a battle or a betrayal — it's demographics, and it takes six centuries to finish. Tolkien states it plainly in Appendix A, and it's worth laying out one link at a time, because no single step looks like it should end a royal line.

1447
Eldacar is restored. To rebuild a kingdom gutted by war, he resettles Gondor with large numbers of his mother's people, the Northmen of Rhovanion — the population, not just the throne, becomes more mixed than before the war that was fought over exactly that question.
1447–
Every king after Eldacar rules knowing his own cousins might raise an army against him. The Kings of Gondor become, in the chronicle's own words, "cautious and watchful of those near of kin."
…
Being watched by your own king is not a comfortable way to live. Over the generations, Dúnedain lords under suspicion take two ways out: some slip away to Umbar and join the families already established in rebellion there; others simply marry outside the old bloodline and quietly stop being candidates for anyone's throne.
…
Each departure and each "lesser" marriage further thins the pure Númenórean line of Anárion — not through war this time, through six hundred years of ordinary people making ordinary choices to avoid royal suspicion.
— six hundred years pass —
2050
King Eärnur rides alone to Minas Morgul to answer the Witch-king's challenge, and is never seen again. The court searches for the next heir of the House of Anárion.
2050
There isn't one. Not "the claim is disputed," as it had been once before, in 1944 — there is no plausible claimant left to dispute. The line that survived a civil war in 1447 has been quietly bled dry by six centuries of its own aftermath.

The Ruling Stewards take over that same year, governing "in the name of the king" because there is, for the first time in Gondor's history, no king to govern in the name of — and won't be for another 969 years, until a descendant of an entirely different line, Isildur's rather than Anárion's, walks out of the wilderness with a claim nobody in Minas Tirith saw coming. The Kin-strife didn't just cost Gondor a city and a century of piracy. It is, on Tolkien's own telling, the reason the kingship itself eventually ran out of people.

Eldacar reigned another forty-three years after Erui, dying peacefully in 1490 — the only person in this whole account who got something like a happy ending. His descendants ruled Gondor for another six hundred years without once managing to have quite enough of them.
